# Do You Need a Permit for a Fence in Tennessee?

There is no single statewide answer. Some cities in Middle Tennessee require a fence permit, some counties ask for a simple form instead, and some places need no paperwork at all as long as the fence follows their height and placement rules. Here is how to find out what applies to your yard before the first post goes in.

## The short answer for most Tennessee fences

Whether you need a fence permit depends on the city or county that covers your address, not on the state. Even where no permit is required, local zoning almost always limits how tall a fence can be, where it can sit on the lot, and what it can block. A fence that breaks those rules can be ordered changed or removed, permit or not.

So the real question is not only "Do I need a permit?" It is "What are the fence rules at my address?" One phone call to the right office answers both.

## Find out which office covers your address

A home inside city limits follows that city's rules. A home outside city limits follows the county. Your mailing address can be misleading here, since plenty of homes with a city name on the mailbox sit in the unincorporated county. Your county property records show which side of the line you are on.

This works the same way it does for decks, which we cover in our guide to [deck permits in Tennessee](/guides/deck-permits-tennessee/). The difference is that fence rules usually live in the zoning code, so the planning or zoning staff may be the ones with the answer, even when the building codes office takes the application.

## Three nearby places, three different answers

Looking at three places side by side shows how much the rules can change from one line on the map to the next. These summaries reflect what each office published as of October 2026. Rules change, so confirm with the office before you build.

### City of Murfreesboro: a permit is required

Murfreesboro treats a fence as an accessory structure, and the city says a permit is required to build, alter, enlarge, move or demolish one. Homeowners can pull their own fence permit for work at their primary residence. The city also lists a few placement rules worth knowing:

* There is no property line setback for fences, but a fence cannot cross onto a neighbor's land.
* Fences are not allowed in drainage or utility easements or in the public right of way, except that the City Engineer may approve a fence in a drainage easement in some cases with a written agreement.
* Keep the fence 3 feet away from mechanical equipment, and do not fence in meters.

Applications go through the Murfreesboro Building and Codes Department at City Hall. The fee is based on the value of the work. If you live in the city, see our page on [fence installation in Murfreesboro](/fence-company-murfreesboro-tn/).

### Unincorporated Rutherford County: a form instead of a permit

Outside city limits in Rutherford County, the county says a building permit is not required for a fence up to 7 feet tall. It does require homeowners to fill out a Fence Design Guideline Affidavit with the Rutherford County Planning Department before installation starts. That makes it easy to skip by accident, so put it on the list early.

### Metro Nashville: a permit for solid fences, firm height rules

In February 2026, Metro Council passed an ordinance that requires a permit from Metro Codes before you build a new opaque fence in Nashville and most of Davidson County. That means a fence that blocks more than half the view, such as a solid wood privacy fence. Open styles such as chain link are not covered, and neither are lots zoned AR, AG, R80 or RS80. Metro's fence web page has not been updated yet, so call Metro Codes before you build to confirm the current permit steps and fee. The rules on where a fence can go are specific:

| Where the fence sits | Maximum height listed by Metro Codes |
|---|---|
| Solid fence within 10 feet of the street right of way | 30 inches |
| Open fence, such as chain link or wrought iron, within 10 feet of the street right of way | 72 inches |
| Rest of the required front setback | 6 feet |
| Required side or rear setback | 8 feet |

On most lots, the street right of way starts at the front property line. Metro measures height from the finished grade on the side of the fence with the most exposed height. Metro also requires that cross beams and bracing face the inside of the property, not the street. Corner lots have an added restriction, with no fences allowed in a 35 foot visibility zone. Wood must be painted, stained or preserved, and metal must be made or treated to resist rust. Homes in special overlay districts, such as historic overlays, can have extra steps, including a preservation permit, so ask the Metro Historic Zoning Commission staff, part of the Metro Planning Department, if your home sits in one. If you are in Nashville, our [Nashville fence page](/fence-company-nashville-tn/) covers how we plan around these rules.

## Height limits and the front yard

Nearly every set of local rules treats the front yard differently from the back. Tall privacy fences are usually fine behind the house and along the sides, while front yard fences are kept lower or more open so the street stays visible. If you want privacy close to the street, ask about the exact cutoff before you pick a style. A 6 foot privacy fence that is perfect for the backyard can be out of bounds a few feet forward.

Height is usually measured from the ground at the fence line. On a sloped lot, a stepped fence can run taller at the low end of each section, so ask how your office measures. Our guide on [fencing a sloped yard](/guides/fence-on-a-slope/) explains stepped and racked styles.

## Corner lots and sight lines

If your house sits on a corner, expect extra limits near the intersection. Cities keep a triangle of open space at the corner so drivers can see oncoming traffic, and a solid fence or tall hedge inside that area is usually not allowed. Nashville's 35 foot visibility zone is one example. Other places use different distances, so get the number for your address before you lay out the fence line.

## Easements, utilities, and meters

Many lots in newer Middle Tennessee subdivisions have drainage or utility easements along the back or side lines. Murfreesboro, for example, does not allow fences in drainage or utility easements or the public right of way. The one exception the city lists is a drainage easement, where the City Engineer may approve a fence in some cases with a written agreement. Where a fence is allowed in an easement, the utility or city may still have the right to remove it to reach their lines, and you may be the one paying to put it back.

Your plat or survey shows where easements run. Our guide to [fences and property lines in Tennessee](/guides/fence-property-line-tennessee/) walks through finding corner pins, reading a survey, and deciding how close to the line to build.

Before any digging, Tennessee law calls for a locate request through Tennessee 811. The service asks for at least three working days' notice, and the request stays valid for fifteen calendar days after the start date on the ticket. A fence line can cross gas, water, electric, and cable lines, so this step matters on every job.

## Pool fences follow stricter rules

A fence that serves as the barrier around a swimming pool is held to a much tighter standard than a yard fence, because it exists to keep small children out. As one local example, Rutherford County's posted pool barrier requirements, based on the International Swimming Pool and Spa Code the county has adopted, include:

* A barrier at least 48 inches tall, measured from the ground.
* No more than 2 inches of clearance under the barrier over grass or mulch, or 4 inches over a hard surface.
* Limits on gaps between boards and pickets that depend on the style of fence.
* Pedestrian gates that open outward away from the pool, close on their own, and latch on their own.
* If the latch release sits lower than 54 inches above the ground, it goes on the pool side of the gate, at least 3 inches below the top.

Pool barriers often need their own permit or inspection even where a regular fence does not. If a pool is in the plans now or later, tell your fence builder before the design is set. A privacy fence can usually be built to meet pool rules from the start, which costs far less than changing it afterward.

## HOA approval is a separate step

A city or county answer does not speak for your homeowners association, and HOA approval does not replace a permit. Many neighborhoods around Murfreesboro, Smyrna, and Spring Hill have their own rules on fence height, style, color, and which side faces out. Some only allow certain materials. Our guide to [getting HOA approval for a fence or deck](/guides/hoa-approval-deck-fence/) covers how to put together a submission that gets approved the first time.

## What to ask when you call the codes office

Keep the call short and specific. Describe the fence in plain words, give your address, and ask:

1. Do I need a permit or any form for this fence, and who can apply for it?
2. What is the maximum height in the front, side, and rear yards?
3. Are there limits near the street, a corner, or a driveway?
4. Can the fence go in a drainage or utility easement on my lot?
5. Are there rules about which side faces out, or what materials I can use?
6. Does a pool barrier need its own permit or inspection?

Write down the name of the person you spoke with and the date. If a permit is required, ask how long review usually takes so you can plan the start date.

## Quick answers

### Do I need a permit to replace an old fence?

Often yes, where permits are required at all. Murfreesboro, for example, requires a permit to alter, move, or demolish a fence, not only to build a new one. Replacing a fence is also a good time to check that the old one met the current height and placement rules, since a replacement may have to follow today's rules.

### How tall can a backyard fence be in Middle Tennessee?

It depends on your city or county. Metro Nashville allows up to 8 feet in side and rear setbacks, but other places set different limits, and many HOAs set their own lower limits. Ask your codes office and your HOA for the numbers that apply to your lot.

### Can I build a fence without a permit if my neighbor agrees?

A neighbor's approval does not replace a permit or the zoning rules. It is still smart to talk with your neighbors before the crew arrives, especially when the fence runs near a shared line.

# Repair or Replace? Signs Your Fence Is Done

A tired fence does not always need to come down. Plenty of problems, like a few broken pickets or a gate that drags, are small fixes. Others, like posts rotting off at the ground, mean the fence has reached the end of its life no matter how good the boards look. Here is how to tell the difference in a Middle Tennessee yard.

## Start with the posts

The posts decide whether a wood fence is worth saving. Pickets and rails are cheap and easy to replace. Posts carry the whole fence, they sit in wet ground, and replacing them means digging. If the posts are sound, most other problems are repairs. If many posts are failing, new boards on old posts will not last.

Walk the fence line and push firmly on each post near the top. A solid post barely moves. A post that rocks, leans, or feels soft is a problem. Then look at the base, right where the post meets the dirt or concrete. That narrow band stays wet longer than any other part of the fence, and it is where rot almost always starts.

Poke the wood at the ground line with a screwdriver. If the tip sinks in easily or the wood crumbles, the post is rotting from the inside, even if it looks fine above.

## Why posts fail in Middle Tennessee ground

Much of our area sits on clay that holds water after a rain and shrinks as it dries in the summer. That cycle keeps the bottom of a post damp for long stretches and can loosen it in the ground over time. Concrete footings help hold a post in place, but water can collect where the wood meets the concrete, which speeds up rot at exactly the spot you cannot see.

Lumber choice matters too. Fence posts set in the ground need lumber rated for ground contact. The end tag on pressure treated lumber shows its rating, and a post made from lumber meant only for use above ground can rot early. Our guide on [cedar vs pressure treated pine](/guides/cedar-vs-pressure-treated-fence/) explains why the posts matter more than the pickets.

## Leaning sections and what causes them

A fence that leans is telling you something about what is underneath. Common causes include:

* **A rotted post.** The post breaks or bends at the ground line and the section tips.
* **A shallow post.** Posts that were not set deep enough work loose over time, especially in soft clay.
* **Wind load.** Solid privacy fences catch a lot of wind, and strong spring storms push hard on them.
* **Water and erosion.** Runoff along the fence line can wash soil away from the posts on a slope.
* **Tree roots and trunks.** A growing tree can push a fence over a few inches at a time.

One or two leaning posts can often be replaced or reset, and the sections rehung. When a whole run leans the same way, look for a shared cause, like a slope that drains along the fence or posts that were all set too shallow. Fixing one post at a time may not hold.

## Loose or broken pickets are usually a repair

Pickets crack, warp, and pop loose, especially on the sunny side of the fence. Swapping out a handful of pickets or refastening loose ones with exterior rated screws is a simple repair. The same goes for a rail that has split in one section.

Look at why the boards are failing, though. If pickets are rotting at the bottom, they may be sitting on the ground or buried in mulch. Boards that touch the soil stay wet and rot quickly. A small gap between the bottom of the pickets and the ground helps them last much longer, and a rot board along the bottom can take the abuse instead of the pickets.

## Sagging gates

A gate that drags on the ground or will not latch is one of the most common fence complaints, and it is often fixable. Gates sag for a few reasons:

1. **The hinge post is leaning.** A gate pulls hard on its post. If that post was not set deep enough or is rotting, the whole gate drops. This calls for a new or stronger post.
2. **The gate frame is racking.** A wood gate frame can slowly fold into a parallelogram. A diagonal brace running from the bottom hinge corner up to the top latch corner, or a cable and turnbuckle kit, can square it up.
3. **The hinges are too light or loose.** Heavier hinges and longer screws, or through bolts, often solve this.

If you replace the gate, consider a metal gate frame for wide openings. Our guide on choosing the [best fence for dogs](/guides/best-fence-for-dogs/) also covers gates that close and latch on their own.

## Storm and tree damage

Middle Tennessee gets strong spring thunderstorms and the occasional tornado, and fallen limbs are a common cause of sudden fence damage. After a storm, check for snapped posts, sections pushed out of line, and gates that no longer close.

Some homeowners insurance policies cover fence damage from wind or falling trees, and some do not, or have limits. Take photos before anything is moved or cleaned up, then check your policy or call your agent before you schedule work. If a neighbor's tree caused the damage, the insurance questions can get more involved, so talk with your agent first.

## Termites, carpenter bees, and other pests

Termites are active in Tennessee and can hollow out posts from the inside. Mud tubes on a post or soft, layered wood at the base are warning signs. Carpenter bees leave round holes in exposed wood, often in the top rail, and woodpeckers sometimes follow them. A few holes can be filled, but heavy damage in the rails can weaken the fence.

## Matching new wood to an old fence

New boards will not match weathered ones right away. Fresh pressure treated pine often has a green or tan color and takes time to gray, while cedar starts a warmer color. If you are repairing a highly visible section, plan to stain the whole run once the new wood has dried, or expect the patch to stand out for a while. Our guide to [privacy fence options](/guides/privacy-fence-options/) covers styles if you are thinking about a change.

## When replacing makes more sense

There is no single number that decides it, but replacement usually wins when several of these are true:

* Many posts are rotting or loose, not just one or two.
* Long runs lean or wave instead of a single section.
* The fence no longer keeps a dog or kids in, or fails pool barrier rules.
* You are fixing something new every season.
* The fence no longer meets your HOA rules, or you want a different height or style.
* You are selling the house and the fence is one of the first things buyers see.

A useful test is to compare the repair estimate with the cost of a new fence. If the repair costs a large share of a replacement and still leaves old posts in the ground, replacement is usually the better value. Our guide to [what drives the cost of a fence](/guides/fence-cost-middle-tennessee/) explains how fence bids are put together.

## Permits, HOA rules, and the property line

Repairing a few pickets rarely involves anyone else. Replacing a fence often does. Some cities require a permit to alter or rebuild a fence, and many HOAs want to approve the new design even if it matches the old one. A replacement is also the right time to confirm the property line, since an old fence is not always in the right place. Start with our guides on [fence permits in Tennessee](/guides/fence-permits-tennessee/) and [fences and property lines in Tennessee](/guides/fence-property-line-tennessee/).

## Quick answers

### How long does a wood fence last in Tennessee?

It depends on the lumber, how the posts were set, drainage along the fence line, and upkeep. Posts usually decide the lifespan. A fence with ground contact rated posts set deep in well drained soil will outlast one with shallow posts sitting in wet clay.

### Can I replace just the posts and keep my pickets?

Sometimes. If the pickets and rails are in good shape, a crew can replace failing posts and rehang the sections. If most of the posts are going, the labor often comes close to building new, and the old boards may not survive being taken down.

### Should I repair or replace a fence before selling my house?

A leaning or broken fence is one of the first things buyers notice. Small repairs are usually worth doing. If the fence is near the end of its life, get a repair price and a replacement price and weigh them against your timeline.

# Deck Railing and Stair Code Basics in Tennessee

Railings and stairs protect people from falls, so they get some of the closest attention during inspections. The rules are not complicated once you see them laid out. Here are the basics most Middle Tennessee codes offices work from, and the questions to ask before your deck is drawn.

## Where these rules come from

Most cities and counties in Tennessee that enforce building codes base their residential rules on the International Residential Code, a national model code, with local changes. Codes offices around Middle Tennessee are not all on the same edition of that code, and each one can add its own amendments. The numbers below are the common baseline that appears in local handouts across the state, but your codes office has the final word. Where the State Fire Marshal's Office handles residential permits, the state uses the 2018 International Residential Code, and its state amendments do not change the stair, railing, or handrail rules. Cities and counties that run their own codes departments can make their own changes, so riser, tread, and handrail numbers can still differ from what you read here. Ask which edition your office uses and whether it has any local changes to railings or stairs.

If you are still figuring out whether your deck needs a permit at all, start with our guide to [deck permits in Tennessee](/guides/deck-permits-tennessee/).

## When a deck needs a railing

Code calls a deck railing a guard. Under the common rule, a guard is required on open sides of a deck or porch when the walking surface is more than 30 inches above the floor or ground below. Recent editions of the model code measure that drop at any point within 36 inches out from the edge, so a deck that sits low near the house but over a slope at the outer edge can still need a guard.

Even when a low deck does not require a railing, many homeowners add one anyway for kids, pets, or older family members. That choice is yours, but if you add one, build it to the same standard.

## How tall a deck railing must be

On a deck, porch, or raised floor that requires a guard, the common minimum height is **36 inches**, measured from the deck surface to the top of the railing. Along the open side of a stair, guards are measured from the line of the stair nosings, and the common minimum there is **34 inches**.

Those are minimums. Some homeowners prefer a taller railing on a high deck, and some railing systems come in 42 inch heights. Check with your HOA as well, since some neighborhoods set rules on railing style and color.

## Baluster spacing

The gaps in a railing are limited so a small child cannot slip through. The common rules are written as the size of a sphere that must not pass through any opening:

| Location | Opening must not pass a sphere of |
|---|---|
| Deck and porch guards | 4 inches |
| Guards on the open sides of stair treads | 4 3/8 inches |
| The triangle formed by the riser, tread, and bottom rail of a stair guard | 6 inches |

In practice, that means balusters set close enough that a 4 inch ball will not fit between them. Keep an eye on the gap under the bottom rail too. It counts as an opening.

## How strong a railing has to be

A railing that looks right can still fail if it is not attached well. Local handouts commonly require guards and handrails to resist a concentrated load of **200 pounds** applied at any point. That force is what someone leaning or falling against the railing can put on it.

Most of that strength comes from how the posts attach to the deck frame. Some codes offices hand out the American Wood Council's prescriptive deck guide, known as DCA 6, which shows tested ways to fasten guard posts to the joists and rim joist. A post held on with a couple of nails or screws through a thin rim joist is a common weak spot on older decks.

Manufactured railing systems, such as aluminum, composite, or cable railings, are usually tested as a complete assembly. Install them exactly as the maker's instructions say, including the post mounts and blocking. Mixing parts or skipping blocking can void the tested strength.

## Stair rules

Deck stairs follow the same basic rules as stairs inside a house. The common baseline includes:

* **Width:** at least 36 inches.
* **Risers:** no more than 7 3/4 inches tall.
* **Treads:** at least 10 inches deep.
* **Consistency:** risers and treads that stay nearly the same size from top to bottom, since an odd step is what trips people.
* **Headroom:** at least 6 feet 8 inches of clear height above the stairs.

Stairs also need solid ground at the bottom. The residential code calls for a floor or landing at the top and bottom of every stairway. The landing must be at least as wide as the stairs and, on a straight run, at least 36 inches deep in the direction of travel. The code lets interior stairs skip the top landing in some cases, but that exception does not cover outdoor deck stairs. A stringer resting on loose gravel or on a paver that settles into the clay will pull the stairs out of level over time, so plan a solid landing or pad at the bottom from the start.

## Handrails on deck stairs

A handrail is commonly required on at least one side of any stair with **four or more risers**. The usual rules for the handrail itself:

* **Height:** between **34 and 38 inches**, measured straight up from the line of the stair nosings.
* **Grip:** a shape a hand can wrap around. A round rail is commonly allowed between 1 1/4 and 2 inches across. A flat 2x6 cap on top of a railing usually does not count as a graspable handrail.
* **Continuity:** the rail runs the full length of the stair.
* **Ends:** returned to a post or wall, or ended in a newel post, so clothing and bags do not catch on it.
* **Wall clearance:** at least 1 1/2 inches between a wall and the rail.

## Lighting at the stairs

The residential code requires a light at the top landing of outdoor stairs (Section R303.8 in the 2018 and 2021 editions). The code text does not say where the switch must go, so ask your codes office what it accepts. Electrical work usually needs its own permit, so plan the light with the rest of the deck instead of adding it later.

## Older decks and new railings

If you are replacing railings or stairs on an existing deck, expect the new work to follow today's rules. Railings built years ago often have wider baluster gaps or shorter heights than current code allows. A railing or stair rebuild is a good time to look at the rest of the structure too, especially the ledger connection to the house. Our guide on [signs your deck is done](/guides/signs-you-need-a-new-deck/) covers what to check.

## Questions to ask before your deck is drawn

1. Which edition of the residential code does your office use, and are there local changes to guards or stairs?
2. Does my deck height require a guard on every side, including over a slope?
3. What will the landing at the bottom of the stairs be made of, and how large does it need to be?
4. Does the stair need a handrail on one side or both?
5. What kind of stair light and switch will you accept, and will it need an electrical permit?
6. Does my HOA set rules on railing height, style, or color?

## Quick answers

### How high does a deck have to be to need a railing?

Under the common rule, a guard is required where the deck surface is more than 30 inches above the ground below, measured within 36 inches of the edge in recent editions of the code. Confirm the rule with your codes office, since local amendments can differ.

### How far apart can deck balusters be?

The common rule is that a 4 inch sphere must not pass through any opening in a deck guard. On the open sides of stair treads the limit is 4 3/8 inches, and in the triangle at the bottom of a stair guard it is 6 inches.

### Do deck stairs need a handrail?

A handrail is commonly required on at least one side of any stair with four or more risers, mounted between 34 and 38 inches above the stair nosings. Shorter stairs may not require one, but a handrail is still a good idea.

# How to Choose a Deck or Fence Builder in Tennessee

A deck or fence is a big check to write, and most of the trouble people run into comes from who they hired, not what they built. Tennessee has clear rules on licenses, deposits, and contracts that protect homeowners. Here is what the law says, how to check a builder in a few minutes, and the questions that separate a careful crew from a risky one.

## Start with the license the job needs

In Tennessee, the license a builder needs depends on the total price of your project, including materials and labor, and on the county you live in.

* **Projects of $25,000 or more** require a state contractor's license. The Tennessee Attorney General's office notes that the license is required before bidding or price negotiations on a job that size.
* **Projects from $3,000 to $24,999** require a state home improvement license in the nine counties that have adopted the home improvement law: Bradley, Davidson, Hamilton, Haywood, Knox, Marion, Robertson, Rutherford, and Shelby. Fencing is one of the types of work this license covers.

That means a fence or deck in Murfreesboro, Smyrna, La Vergne, or anywhere else in Rutherford County, or in Nashville and the rest of Davidson County, falls under the home improvement law when the price lands in that range. In other counties, smaller projects may not need a state license, but cities and counties can still require a local business license.

A state license depends on the price of the job and the county, not on whether the project needs a permit. Electrical work, like deck lighting or outlets, can require its own license and permit no matter the project size.

## How to check a license in a few minutes

Tennessee runs a free license lookup at [verify.tn.gov](https://verify.tn.gov). Search the business name or the license number from the bid. Check that:

* The license is active, not expired or suspended.
* The name on the license matches the company on your contract.
* The license type fits the job and its price.

For complaint history, the Board for Licensing Contractors takes calls at (615) 741 8307. You can also confirm the business is registered with the Tennessee Secretary of State.

## Ask for proof of insurance

Two kinds of coverage matter most.

**Workers' compensation.** Tennessee requires construction businesses with one or more employees to carry workers' compensation insurance. Owners must also carry coverage on themselves unless they are listed on the state's exemption registry. If a worker is hurt on your property and the builder is not covered, the claim can get complicated, so ask.

**General liability.** This covers damage the work causes, like a cracked driveway, a broken window, or a cut utility line.

Ask for a certificate of insurance sent directly from the insurance agent, not a photocopy, and check that the dates cover your project.

## Know the deposit rules

Tennessee law limits deposits on home improvement work. Under the home improvement section of Tennessee law (Title 62, Chapter 6 of the Tennessee Code), a licensed home improvement contractor generally cannot take more than one third of the contract price as a deposit at or before signing. The law allows exceptions, such as when the contractor provides a performance and payment bond, or when you are informed in writing of your right to hold the final payment until the work is done and you agree to a different payment schedule.

The same law prohibits demanding or taking any payment before a written contract is signed. The Attorney General's office adds two practical tips: never let your payments get ahead of the work that is finished, and never pay in cash.

A reasonable payment plan usually ties each payment to a finished stage, such as posts set, framing inspected, or final walkthrough complete.

## Get everything in a written contract

The Attorney General's office urges homeowners to get everything in writing. A good contract spells out:

* The builder's full business name, address, and license number.
* A clear description of the work and the materials, including brand, grade, and color where it matters.
* Start and completion dates, even if approximate.
* The total price and the payment schedule.
* How changes are handled. Changes should be priced and signed in writing before the changed work starts.
* Who pulls the permits and who handles HOA approval.
* Cleanup, hauling away the old deck or fence, and protection of your lawn and driveway.
* The workmanship warranty, and the manufacturer warranties on materials.

Do not sign a contract with blank spaces. If something you discussed is not on paper, it is not part of the deal.

## Ask who pulls the permits

A builder who will not pull permits, or asks you to pull them so the job goes faster, is a red flag. The Attorney General's office lists reluctance to pull permits among the warning signs to watch for. Permits mean an inspector checks the footings, framing, and railings at the stages where problems are easy to fix. Our guide to [deck permits in Tennessee](/guides/deck-permits-tennessee/) explains how inspections work, and our guide to [fence permits in Tennessee](/guides/fence-permits-tennessee/) covers how fence rules vary by city.

## Questions that show how a builder thinks

Good builders welcome specific questions. A few that reveal a lot:

1. **What happens if you hit rock?** Limestone sits close to the surface in much of the Central Basin. A careful builder plans for it and explains how it is priced.
2. **How deep do the footings or posts go, and why?** The answer should reference local code and soil, not a habit.
3. **How does the deck attach to the house?** Listen for the ledger, flashing, and the right fasteners. A deck pulling away from the house is a common way decks fail.
4. **What fasteners and hangers do you use with treated lumber?** Modern treated lumber can corrode hardware that is not rated for it.
5. **Who will be on site, and who do I call with a problem?**
6. **Do you call Tennessee 811 before digging?** The answer should be yes, every time.
7. **What does your warranty cover, and for how long?** Get it in writing.
8. **Can I see recent jobs or talk with past customers?**

## Compare bids line by line

Two bids for the same yard can look far apart until you line them up. Check that both include the same materials, the same height and length, gates, tear out, permits, and cleanup. A low bid that leaves out permits or debris removal is not really lower. Our guides to [what drives the cost of a deck](/guides/deck-cost-middle-tennessee/) and [what drives the cost of a fence](/guides/fence-cost-middle-tennessee/) walk through how to compare bids side by side.

## Red flags to walk away from

* Pressure to sign today, or a "special price" that expires tonight.
* A demand for full payment or a large cash deposit up front.
* No written contract, or a contract with blanks.
* No license number, or a license that does not match the business name.
* Reluctance to pull permits.
* A door to door offer after a storm from a company you cannot verify.

## Quick answers

### Does a fence builder need a license in Tennessee?

It depends on the price and the county. In the nine counties that adopted the home improvement law, including Davidson and Rutherford, fencing priced from $3,000 to $24,999 requires a home improvement license. Any project of $25,000 or more requires a state contractor's license.

### How much deposit should I pay a deck or fence builder?

Under Tennessee law, a licensed home improvement contractor generally cannot take more than one third of the contract price as a deposit, with limited exceptions. No payment should be made before a written contract is signed.

### Should the builder or the homeowner pull the permit?

In most cases the builder should. Some cities, such as Murfreesboro, allow homeowners to pull permits for their own primary residence, but a builder who refuses to pull permits is a warning sign.

# Best Time to Build a Deck or Fence in Tennessee

In Middle Tennessee you can build a deck or fence in almost any month. The season still shapes how smoothly the job goes, how long you wait for a crew, and when you can stain the wood. The best time to build usually comes down to one thing: starting the planning early enough that the build lands when you want to use it.

## The short answer

Fall is often the easiest season to build. The ground is usually workable, the heat has broken, and many crews have more room on the schedule than in spring. Spring is the most popular time, which means longer waits and more rain delays. Summer works but brings heat and storms. Winter is a fine time to plan, pull permits, and get HOA approval, and many jobs can be built then too.

If you want a deck ready for Memorial Day or a fence up before a new puppy comes home in May, the real deadline is a few months earlier, when the planning should start.

## What each season brings

### Spring

Spring is when most people decide they want a new deck or fence, so schedules fill fast. Middle Tennessee also gets strong spring thunderstorms, and heavy rain is hard on a job that depends on open holes in the ground. Footing and post holes fill with water, the clay turns to mud, and concrete waits until the holes drain. Inspections can back up too.

None of that makes spring a bad time to build. It just means padding the schedule and booking early.

### Summer

Long days help crews get more done, and the ground is usually firmer. Heat is the tradeoff. Afternoon storms can stop work quickly, and crews often start early to beat the worst of the heat. New lumber also dries and moves more in hot weather, which is part of why boards can shrink or cup in the first few months.

Summer is also yard season. Plan for where materials and equipment will sit, and for a few days when part of the yard is off limits.

### Fall

Fall often brings mild temperatures and fewer of the heavy storms that slow spring jobs. Many homeowners are thinking about the holidays instead of the backyard, so crews may have shorter wait times. A deck or fence built in fall is ready the first warm weekend of spring.

Falling leaves are the main nuisance. They hide the ground, and wet leaves left on new boards can stain them, so ask the crew to keep the work area clear.

### Winter

Our winters are usually mild enough to keep building through most of the season. Cold snaps and frozen ground do happen, and fresh concrete should not freeze while it cures, so a crew may pause or protect the footings during a hard freeze. Short days mean fewer working hours.

Winter shines as a planning season. You can get estimates, settle on materials, submit to your HOA, and pull permits while everyone else waits for spring, then be first on the schedule when the weather turns.

## Work backward from the date you want

The build itself is often the shortest part of the timeline. Here is what usually comes before it.

1. **Estimates and design.** Allow time to meet with builders, compare bids, and choose materials. Our guides to [what drives the cost of a deck](/guides/deck-cost-middle-tennessee/) and [what drives the cost of a fence](/guides/fence-cost-middle-tennessee/) break down the numbers.
2. **HOA approval.** Many review committees meet only on a set schedule, and some want changes before they approve. Our guide to [getting HOA approval for a fence or deck](/guides/hoa-approval-deck-fence/) covers how to get it right the first time.
3. **Permits.** Review time varies by city and county and by season. Ask your codes office for a typical turnaround. See our guides to [deck permits in Tennessee](/guides/deck-permits-tennessee/) and [fence permits in Tennessee](/guides/fence-permits-tennessee/).
4. **Materials.** Common lumber is usually easy to get. Specific composite colors, railing systems, or custom gates can take longer to arrive.
5. **Utility locates.** Tennessee 811 asks for at least three working days' notice before digging, and the request is valid for fifteen calendar days after the start date on the ticket.
6. **Inspections.** A deck usually has a footing inspection, a framing inspection, and a final inspection. Weather and inspector schedules can add a few days between them.

Add those up and a spring deck can easily start in winter. A fence is often simpler, but HOA review alone can add weeks.

## When to stain or seal new wood

Many people plan to stain right after the build, but new pressure treated lumber is often too wet to take stain well. It usually needs time to dry first, and the right wait depends on the lumber, the weather, and the product, so read the stain maker's directions. Most stains also list a temperature range on the can and need a dry stretch after application.

That makes a fall build convenient. The wood can dry over the winter and be stained in spring. A spring build can often be stained in late summer or fall. Our guide to [deck care in Tennessee weather](/guides/deck-maintenance-tennessee/) covers sealing, staining, and mildew.

Composite decking does not need stain, which makes timing simpler for that part of the job.

## Decks and fences time differently

A fence is usually faster to build than a deck, and some places that require a deck permit do not require a building permit for a fence. Goodlettsville is one example. The biggest timing factors for a fence are HOA approval, the property line, and how much rock is under the fence line. A deck takes more planning and inspections, so it benefits most from starting early.

If you are doing both, building the deck first can make sense, since the fence layout may change around new stairs or a gate. Talk it through with your builder.

## Quick answers

### Can you build a deck in the winter in Tennessee?

Usually, yes. Middle Tennessee winters are mild enough for most of the season. A crew may pause during a hard freeze, since fresh concrete in the footings should not freeze while it cures, and staining waits for warmer weather.

### When should I start planning a deck for summer?

Start in winter. Estimates, design choices, HOA review, and permits can take weeks before any digging starts, and spring schedules fill quickly.

### Is fall a good time to put up a fence?

Yes. Fall often brings mild weather and fewer heavy storms than spring, and crews may have shorter waits. A fence built in fall can dry over the winter and be stained in spring.
